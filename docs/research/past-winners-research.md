# Past NASA Space Apps global winners (2021 to 2025) and lessons for Zenacor

Written Sat Nov 14, 2026 for the Lunar Landing Site Navigator submission (deadline Sun Nov 15, 11:59pm Arizona time).

## How far to trust this

- **Verified:** the winner lists, awards, challenges and one-line descriptions for all five years (50 projects), taken from NASA and spaceappschallenge.org announcement pages. The judging criteria, award definitions and submission rules, taken from the official guides.
- **Not verified:** the individual project pages (`/find-a-team/.../?tab=project`). The site blocks automated readers from those pages, so I could not open any of them. That means each winner's demo format (video or slides), repo link, and write-up structure are **not confirmed per project**. Where a format is mentioned below it comes from the announcement text. If a teammate has ten minutes, opening three or four of the linked project pages by hand is the single best follow-up (suggested picks in section 6).
- **Why each one won** is my inference from the award definition plus the description. NASA does not publish judges' reasons.
- The official guides still carry 2024 and 2025 dates in places. The 2026 home page confirms Nov 14 9:00am to Nov 15 11:59pm local time. Confirm anything rule-related with the Local Lead.

## 1. Rules and judging, as published

**Five judging criteria** ([Judging & Awards Guide](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/judging-awards-guide)):

| Criterion | The question judges ask |
|---|---|
| Influence | How much influence can this have? Big problem or little? Helps many or few? |
| Creativity | Novel, or "an incremental improvement on something that already exists"? |
| Validity | Scientifically valid? Will it do what it sets out to do? Can it work in the real world? |
| Relevance | Responsive to the challenge? Complete or a long way to go? Feasible? Usable? |
| Presentation | Did the team tell the story: the challenge, the solution, why it matters? |

Note the first criterion is named **Influence** on the current guide. Our plan file calls it Impact, which was the older name.

**Three phases:** local judges pick Global Nominees, NASA and partner-agency experts pick Global Finalists (40 in 2023), then an executive committee picks one winner per award.

**Ten global awards**, with the official definitions:

| Award | Definition |
|---|---|
| Best Use of Science | Best and most valid use of the scientific method |
| Best Use of Data | Best makes space data accessible, or leverages it to a unique application |
| Best Use of Technology | Most innovative use of technology |
| Galactic Impact | Most potential to improve life on Earth or in the universe |
| Best Mission Concept | Most plausible concept and design |
| Most Inspirational | Captures our hearts |
| Best Storytelling | Most creatively communicates the potential of open data through storytelling |
| Global Connection | Best connects people around the world through technology |
| Art & Technology | Most effectively combines technical and creative skills |
| Local Impact | Greatest potential to make a difference in the local community |

**Submission rules that can disqualify** ([Project Submission Guide](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/project-submission-guide/), 2025 edition):

- Demo is a video of **at most 30 seconds** or a deck of **at most 7 slides including the title**. Over the limit means not eligible for Global Judging.
- Demo link must be public with no login or permission request. Video needs English subtitles. Everything in English.
- **Must use NASA data or resources** to be eligible for a global award, and must list every dataset, tool, library, image and piece of code used, including open-source ones.
- AI use must be declared: where and how. AI images and video need a visible watermark. AI-written code must be acknowledged in the text. No AI-altered NASA logos.
- Form fields: project name, high-level summary, demo link, final project link, detailed description, NASA data, partner and other data, use of AI.
- Late saves are not judged and the submission is final.

## 3. Patterns across the 50 winners

1. **Interactive web apps dominate the technical awards.** Almost every Best Use of Science, Data, Technology and Mission Concept winner is a working web tool or a data pipeline with a visual front end. Our format is the right one.
2. **Winners turn data into a decision.** "2plant" answers plant or don't plant. SkySense gives the safest time. SHINE gives savings. Landsat Connect notifies you. The winners rarely stop at "here is a visualization". They end at a recommendation.
3. **Many winners go one step past the brief.** SkyShield was asked for an orrery and delivered a risk tool. Resonant Exoplanets added biosignatures. PureFlow added storm stress tests. The Creativity criterion explicitly penalizes "an incremental improvement on something that already exists".
4. **One memorable hook.** "Credit score for satellites." "Plant or don't plant." A project that can be said in six words is easier to advance through three rounds of tired judges.
5. **3D Moon and orbit tools have won repeatedly:** Selene and Brute Force (2022), LunarTech Ensemble and Spacebee (2023), the ISS 3D tracker (2022), Cambridge Asteroids with Three.js (2021), PureFlow (2025). Two of these teams said it was their first 3D project. Polish matters less than a clear interaction: pick a date, see what happens.
6. **Validity shows up as numbers.** Storm Prophet's coverage quotes an accuracy figure. Best Use of Science is defined around the scientific method. A stated check against a reference is rare among hackathon entries and stands out.
7. **Small and inexperienced teams win.** A solo entrant won Best Use of Science in 2025. WMPGang had no web experience. Scope and clarity beat feature count.
8. **The same challenge can win several awards.** Moonquake Map produced three winners in 2022 and two in 2023. Commercializing LEO produced three in 2025. Sharing a challenge with strong entries, or with LunaNav, does not block a win. Each winner had a different angle.
9. **Presentation is one fifth of the score.** A 2017 official post says the best teams give it as much attention as the other four, and recommends a teammate dedicated to story and design ([source](https://2017.spaceappschallenge.org/blog/how-develop-winning-space-apps-solution)). The 2021 Galactic Impact team listed video editing and script writing among its skills.

## 4. Lessons for our submission, in priority order

1. **Make the tool give an answer.** For a chosen site and date window, output a ranked recommendation with reasons, such as "Site A, Dec 3 to 9: 6.2 days of continuous sunlight, Earth above the horizon 100% of the time". The 3D view then explains the answer. This is the difference between a viewer and a navigator, and it is what the Decision pattern above rewards.
2. **Show a validation number.** Compare our Sun and Earth elevation and azimuth against JPL Horizons for a few sites and dates, and put the error in the write-up and on a slide ("agrees with Horizons to within X degrees"). This targets Validity directly and is our best claim to Best Use of Science. Do not publish a number we have not computed.
3. **Pick one thing LunaNav does not do and name it.** Candidates from our own brainstorm: side-by-side comparison of two sites on the same dates, a mission-window finder that searches dates, a shareable link, or local terrain horizon effects. One finished differentiator beats three partial ones. Credit LunaNav openly in the data and tools list if we use any of its numbers. Hiding prior art is riskier than citing it.
4. **Lock the 30-second demo early.** Suggested script: 0 to 5s the problem ("Where and when do you land at the lunar south pole?"), 5 to 22s screen recording of one flow (pick two sites, scrub the date, Sun and Earth move, recommendation appears), 22 to 30s the validation number and NASA data sources. Add English subtitles. Keep it at 29 seconds. Host on YouTube as unlisted or public and test the link in a private window.
5. **Deploy a live link.** A public URL (Vercel fits Next.js) as the "final project" link lets judges at all three rounds try it. Deployment needs Ryan's yes under our rules.
6. **Write the project page for skimmers.** High-level summary in two or three sentences with the hook first. Then sections in this order: the challenge, what it does, how it works, data and validation, what is next. Put screenshots in. Answer each of the five criteria somewhere explicitly.
7. **List NASA data precisely.** Name each dataset and tool (for example LRO LOLA elevation, JPL Horizons or SPICE kernels, the USGS Artemis III region shapefiles) with links. No NASA data listed means no global award.
8. **Declare AI use honestly and specifically.** Code written with AI assistance must be acknowledged in the text. Watermark any AI images or video.
9. **Award fit.** Aim the write-up at **Best Use of Data** (makes space data accessible) and **Best Mission Concept** (most plausible concept and design), with Best Use of Science as a stretch if the validation is solid. Teams do not choose the award, but the write-up can make the fit obvious.
10. **Protect time.** Past winners advise sleeping, building on existing libraries, and accepting an unfinished feature list. Submit an early saved draft well before 11:59pm, because late saves are not judged.
